** Identity & forms
- Boron is a trace metalloid ingested mainly from fruit, vegetables, nuts, wine and drinking water. Fruit, wine and nuts were key dietary determinants of urinary boron in one cohort [PMID:34677681]. Typical Western intake is about 1-1.35 mg/day [PMID:11248136].
- Inorganic borates (boric acid, sodium tetraborate/borax, sodium pentaborate) exist in the body almost entirely as undissociated boric acid because of its high pKa [PMID:32170343]. Oral boric acid is readily and almost completely absorbed, distributes in body water and is not metabolised [PMID:10050928].
- Supplement forms used in human trials:
  - Calcium fructoborate (CFB): a sugar-borate ester with at most 2.9% boron, about 4.7% calcium and 84.2% fructose. It hydrolyses fully under simulated GI conditions, so EFSA assessed it as boron [PMID:34257728]. Proponents argue that sugar-borate complexes act partly as "microbiota-accessible" boron. This is a hypothesis from a review with a CFB-industry author [PMID:41463367].
  - Boron citrate [PMID:35183882, PMID:40792045]
  - Boric acid [PMID:40792045]
  - Sodium tetraborate decahydrate [PMID:12428176]
  - Boron glycinate and aspartate chelates are sold commercially, but no trial using them specifically was found. Assume they behave like other borates once hydrolysed. This is an inference, not a cited fact.
- Regulatory status: the US Institute of Medicine (IOM) set neither an EAR, RDA nor AI for boron, because a clear human biological function has not been established [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15]. Human essentiality remains unproven according to a 2026 critical review [PMID:42579050].

** Mechanisms
- Boric acid forms reversible complexes with cis-diol molecules. This is the hypothesised basis for its interactions with NAD+-, S-adenosylmethionine- and glycoconjugate-related pathways (mechanistic) [PMID:10050928, PMID:42579050].
- Steroid hormones: one hypothesis is that boron uncouples steroid hormones from blood transport proteins. In the uncontrolled study by Naghii et al. (n=8 men), SHBG fell within 6 h of 10 mg boron; after 1 week, free testosterone rose and estradiol fell [PMID:21129941]. In postmenopausal women eating a low-boron diet, 3 mg/day boron raised serum 17beta-estradiol and testosterone [PMID:3678698].
- Mineral metabolism: in metabolic-ward studies of postmenopausal women, 3 mg/day boron reduced urinary calcium and magnesium excretion [PMID:3678698]. The direction of the calcium effect depended on magnesium status [PMID:9062533]. Plasma boron rose only 1.5-fold with a 9-fold intake increase, and excretion matched intake, suggesting homeostatic control [PMID:9062533].
- Vitamin D: in vitamin D-deficient rats, supplemental boron increased apparent calcium, magnesium and phosphorus balance (animal) [PMID:7889882]. In the small human study by Naghii et al., 25(OH)D rose after 1 week [PMID:21129941].
- Inflammation: hsCRP and TNF-alpha fell after boron supplementation in the uncontrolled study [PMID:21129941]. CFB lowered CRP, fibrinogen and ESR in a pilot RCT [PMID:21607703].
- Gut microbiota: boric acid in drinking water changed gut microbiota composition in mice (animal, pilot) [PMID:39458975].

Overall: no systematic review or meta-analysis of boron supplementation was found. All human trials are small and short, and many are industry-linked or come from one or two research groups. A 2026 critical review concluded that human data are insufficient to justify dietary recommendations or supplementation [PMID:41683210].

** Dosing in trials
- 3 mg/day boron (metabolic-ward studies in postmenopausal women) [PMID:3678698, PMID:9062533]. A narrative review proposed 3 mg/day for bone support [PMID:32540741].
- 2.5 mg/day for 7 weeks (bodybuilders) [PMID:7889885]. 6 mg/day (osteoarthritis) [PMID:7889887].
- 10 mg/day: for 1 week [PMID:21129941]; peri-menstrually for 2 cycles [PMID:25906949]; for 8 weeks as boric acid or boron citrate [PMID:40792045]. 10 mg twice daily for 2 weeks [PMID:36808295].
- Acute single dose of 11.6 mg (sodium tetraborate) [PMID:12428176].
- CFB 110 mg twice daily (220 mg/day, roughly 6 mg boron or less) for 14 days [PMID:24940052, PMID:34257728]. EFSA approved CFB at up to 220 mg/day (at most 6.4 mg boron) for adults, excluding pregnancy and lactation [PMID:34257728].
- Upper limits:
  - IOM UL 20 mg boron/day for adults ≥19 y, including pregnancy and lactation. Lower ULs apply to younger groups: 17 mg (14-18 y), 11 mg (9-13 y), 6 mg (4-8 y) and 3 mg (1-3 y). No UL was set for infants (food and formula only) [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15].
  - The IOM UL derivation was 9.6 mg/kg/day ÷ 30 = 0.3 mg/kg/day. The NOAEL of 9.6 mg/kg/day came from rat developmental toxicity (lower fetal weight). The uncertainty factor of 30 combined 10 for animal-to-human and 3 for intraspecies variation [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15].
  - EFSA's limits are lower: an ADI of 0.16 mg/kg bw/day [PMID:34257728], and an adult UL of 10 mg/day as cited in [PMID:32540741].
- A narrative review noted that no beneficial effects have been reported at intakes above 3 mg/day [PMID:26770156].

** Safety & adverse effects
- At trial doses (≤20 mg/day), no serious adverse events were reported in the RCTs whose abstracts were read [PMID:21607703, PMID:36808295, PMID:40792045].
- Acute toxicity (mostly boric acid ingestion or skin exposure):
  - Fatalities have occurred after oral ingestion and after skin exposure, particularly through damaged skin. Effects include gastrointestinal and skin toxicity. Excretion is mainly urinary [PMID:33485927].
  - A child who ingested an estimated 2.7-8.1 g boric acid had a plasma boron of 16.5 mg/L. He developed nausea, vomiting and headache and recovered with IV hydration; the authors advise hyperhydration and monitoring for ingestions over 3 g [PMID:41120974].
  - A 2026 report described accidental boron poisoning from a dietary supplement. The abstract has no details, so the dose and mechanism could not be verified [PMID:42478460].
- Reproductive and developmental toxicity (animal):
  - In rats, mice and dogs, boron causes dose-dependent testicular toxicity. The male fertility NOAEL in rats is 17.5 mg B/kg/day [PMID:32170343].
  - The developmental NOAEL in rats is 9.6 mg B/kg/day [PMID:32170343].
  - The EU classifies boric acid and borates as reproductive toxicants, category 1B [PMID:37351904].
- Human reproductive data: boron-mining and -processing workers in China and Türkiye had daily boron exposures up to about 41-47 mg/day without reported reproductive or developmental effects. Their blood boron levels stayed well below the rat NOAEL-equivalent levels [PMID:37351904, PMID:32170343].
- Genotoxicity and carcinogenicity: the overall picture suggests borates are not genotoxic, and a 2-year mouse study found no carcinogenicity [PMID:33485927].
- Pharmacokinetics: elimination half-life is about 21 h in humans, with unchanged renal excretion [PMID:10050928].
- Observational mortality signal: in a German cohort, higher plasma boron was associated with higher all-cause mortality in women (HR 1.11 per 5 µg/L; only 27 female deaths) but not in men [PMID:38244065].

** Contraindications
- Pregnancy and lactation (supplemental use): the IOM UL of 20 mg/day nominally applies in pregnancy [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15]. However:
  - The critical toxicity endpoint is developmental [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15].
  - EFSA excluded pregnant and lactating women from its CFB authorisation [PMID:34257728].
  - In an Argentine cohort, higher serum boron in the third trimester was associated with shorter and lighter newborns (0.9 cm and 120 g per 100 µg/L) [PMID:27502898].
  - Renal boron clearance is higher in pregnancy (68.3 vs 54.3 mL/min/1.73 m²) [PMID:11248136].
  - Conclusion: avoid supplemental boron beyond dietary intake.
- Children: ULs are much lower (3-11 mg/day) [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15]. Accidental boric acid ingestion is a recognised hazard [PMID:41120974].
- Hormone-sensitive conditions (e.g. breast, uterine or prostate cancer; endometriosis): boron can shift circulating estradiol and testosterone [PMID:3678698, PMID:21129941]. Caution is therefore theoretical; no outcome data exist. Observational studies link higher dietary boron with lower prostate and lung cancer risk [PMID:15010890, PMID:18343880], so the direction of any clinical effect is unknown.
- Chronic kidney disease / dialysis: boron is cleared almost entirely by the kidneys [PMID:10050928]. In long-term haemodialysis patients, serum boron was above control levels before a session and below them afterwards [PMID:8931348]. Boron is efficiently dialysable (dialysance about 139 mL/min) [PMID:9252976]. A review covers renal effects of acute and chronic boron exposure [PMID:16198928]. Expect accumulation when GFR is reduced.
- Boric acid products (antiseptic powders and vaginal products) are not for oral use. Oral ingestion has caused deaths [PMID:33485927].

** Interactions
*** Drugs
- Estrogen / menopausal hormone therapy:
  - Boron supplementation raised serum 17beta-estradiol in postmenopausal women [PMID:3678698].
  - In women taking estrogen therapy, boron deprivation blunted the estrogen-induced rise in estradiol and plasma copper [PMID:7889883].
  - Effect is possibly additive; the clinical significance is unknown (metabolic-ward studies, n≤14). Classified as "caution" (minor).
- Testosterone or other androgen therapy: free testosterone rose and SHBG fell in an uncontrolled study [PMID:21129941]. This is a theoretical additive effect only.
- Tamsulosin: in the ESWL trial, boron was compared with tamsulosin, not combined with it. No interaction data [PMID:36808295].
- No pharmacokinetic drug interactions were identified. Boron is not metabolised [PMID:10050928].
*** Other supplements
- Magnesium:
  - Boron reduced urinary Mg loss, more so on a low-Mg diet [PMID:3678698].
  - The direction of boron's effect on calcium excretion depended on Mg intake [PMID:9062533].
  - Boron did not change the response to Mg deprivation [PMID:15724868].
  - Combined Mg plus boron outperformed either alone on bone markers in ovariectomised rats (animal) [PMID:41075129].
  - Classified as a plausible complementary effect ("synergy", minor).
- Vitamin D-3: 25(OH)D rose after 1 week of boron in n=8 men [PMID:21129941]. Boron improved mineral balance in vitamin D-deficient rats [PMID:7889882]. Classified as "synergy" (minor; very low certainty).
- Calcium: CFB contains about 4.7% calcium [PMID:34257728], a negligible amount at 220 mg/day. Boron reduced urinary calcium in some studies [PMID:3678698].
- Stacking boron sources (multivitamins, bone formulas, CFB, high-boron mineral water): total intake should be kept below the 20 mg/day UL [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10026/chapter/15]. EFSA's ADI is 0.16 mg/kg/day [PMID:34257728].
*** Conditions
- Pregnancy, chronic kidney disease, and hormone-sensitive cancers: see Contraindications.
*** Lab-test interference
- No cited assay interference was found.
- Blood and urine boron can be measured by ICP-MS/ICP-OES to confirm exposure [PMID:41120974, PMID:38244065].
- Changes in SHBG, estradiol, testosterone and 25(OH)D after supplementation reflect reported physiological changes, not assay artefacts [PMID:21129941, PMID:3678698].

** Latest research
- [2026-08] A critical narrative review found no indispensable boron-dependent human pathway. It identified small samples, short trials, heterogeneous formulations and surrogate endpoints as constraints, and called for speciation-resolved dosing and adequately powered RCTs [PMID:42579050].
- [2026-05] A case report described accidental boron poisoning from a dietary supplement. Details are not available in the abstract [PMID:42478460].
- [2026-05] A handbook review summarised possible roles in bone, osteoarthritis, hormones and inflammation. Boron is not classified as an essential nutrient [PMID:42185672].
- [2026-03] A review of biological effects of boron in animals and humans (no abstract available) [PMID:41886026].
- [2026-01] A critical review of lithium, silicon and boron concluded that human data are insufficient to justify dietary recommendations or supplementation [PMID:41683210].
- [2025-12] A review (one author employed by a CFB manufacturer) proposed a dual framework of plasma-accessible boron vs microbiota-accessible boron complexes. This is a hypothesis [PMID:41463367].
- [2025-10] Combined magnesium plus boron improved BMD, 25(OH)D and bone markers in ovariectomised rats more than either alone (animal) [PMID:41075129].
- [2025-10] A paediatric boric acid ingestion case (plasma B 16.5 mg/L) resolved with hydration [PMID:41120974].
- [2025-08] In an RCT (n=60, 8 weeks), 10 mg/day boric acid or boron citrate did not reduce kidney stone number or size vs placebo [PMID:40792045].
- [2024-10] Boron compounds altered gut microbiota composition in mice (pilot study) [PMID:39458975].
- [2024-01] In a German population cohort (n=863), higher plasma boron was associated with higher mortality in women only. The signal was imprecise [PMID:38244065].

** Open questions
- Do boron supplements improve BMD or reduce fractures? No RCT with hard skeletal outcomes was found [PMID:32540741].
- Are the short-term osteoarthritis symptom benefits of CFB reproducible in independent, longer, adequately powered trials [PMID:24940052, PMID:21607703]?
- Are the hormone effects real and consistent? Results differ between the uncontrolled study and the placebo-controlled trial [PMID:21129941, PMID:7889885, PMID:15724868].
- What is the clinical meaning of the female mortality signal [PMID:38244065] vs the protective associations [PMID:34677681]?
- Is boron essential to humans at all [PMID:42579050]? Do different chemical forms differ in effect [PMID:41463367]?
