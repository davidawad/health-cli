** Identity & forms
- Ginger is the rhizome of Zingiber officinale Roscoe (Zingiberaceae). It is used as a food spice and sold as dried rhizome powder in capsules, teas and standardized extracts [PMID:35147170] [PMID:26228533].
- Main pungent bioactives are gingerols (6-, 8-, 10-gingerol). These dehydrate on heating or drying to shogaols (e.g. 6-shogaol), which are used as quality-control markers [PMID:26228533].
- Pharmacokinetics: after single oral doses of 100 mg-2.0 g, no free gingerols or shogaols were found in plasma, only glucuronide and sulfate conjugates. At 2.0 g, 6-gingerol conjugate Cmax was about 0.85 ug/mL, tmax about 1 h and elimination half-life under 2 h [PMID:18708382].
- Products used in trials:
  - Dried rhizome powder capsules, most often 0.5-2 g/d [PMID:35147170] [PMID:21818642].
  - Standardized extracts, e.g. 84 mg/d gingerols/shogaols [PMID:37699474], 160 mg/d standardized extract [PMID:28666335], and 125 mg/d CO2/fermented extract with 10% gingerols [PMID:40732990].
  - Steamed ginger extract GGE03, standardized to 1-dehydro-6-gingerdione, at 480 mg/d [PMID:41599979] [PMID:40878144] [PMID:39212491].
- Regulatory status: EU HMPC recognizes "well-established use" of powdered rhizome to prevent nausea and vomiting in motion sickness. It lists traditional use for mild spasmodic GI complaints, loss of appetite, minor joint pain and cold symptoms [URL:https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma].
- Products differ widely in content, and poor reporting of interventions limits comparison across trials [PMID:35147170].

** Mechanisms
- Antiemetic action appears to be peripheral and GI rather than central or vestibular:
  - Ginger did not alter vestibular or optokinetic nystagmus, whereas dimenhydrinate did [PMID:2683568].
  - In circular-vection motion sickness, 1-2 g reduced tachygastria and the rise in plasma vasopressin [PMID:12576305].
- Gastric motility: 1.2 g accelerated gastric emptying (half-emptying time 13.1 vs 26.7 min) and increased antral contractions in healthy volunteers [PMID:18403946]. It also sped emptying in functional dyspepsia without changing symptoms or gut peptides [PMID:21218090].
- Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects: in RCT meta-analyses, CRP, TNF-alpha, IL-6 and MDA fall and TAC and SOD rise [PMID:41123858] [PMID:32763761]. 
- Platelet effects: gingerols inhibit arachidonic-acid pathways in vitro. Human studies are split: 4 of 8 clinical trials found reduced platelet aggregation and 4 found no effect [PMID:26488162].
- Drug-metabolism effects (in vitro): ginger extract and its constituents strongly inhibited CYP3A4, 2C9, 1A2 and 2B6, P-gp and BCRP, and activated PXR and AhR. Intestinal concentrations at recommended doses could exceed CYP IC50 values [PMID:37134183].

** Dosing in trials
- Nausea of pregnancy: about 1 g/d for at least 4 days [PMID:11275030]. Lower doses below 1500 mg/d looked better in subgroup analysis [PMID:24642205], and 500-1500 mg/d in divided doses is typical [PMID:40808693].
- CINV:
  - 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 g/d for 6 days starting 3 days before chemotherapy; 0.5-1.0 g gave the largest reduction [PMID:21818642].
  - 84 mg/d gingerols/shogaols for 5 days per cycle [PMID:37699474].
  - 160 mg/d standardized extract was ineffective with high-dose cisplatin [PMID:28666335].
- PONV: usually a single preoperative dose in trials pooled by [PMID:34312974] [PMID:34700257].
- Motion sickness: 1 g powdered rhizome before exposure [PMID:3277342]; 1-2 g in a laboratory model [PMID:12576305].
- Dysmenorrhea: powder taken during the first days of menses in pooled RCTs [PMID:38770631].
- Osteoarthritis: extracts and powders over weeks to months [PMID:25300574]; steamed extract 0.48 g/d for 12 weeks [PMID:39212491].
- Metabolic outcomes: 1.2-2 g/d powder for 4-12 weeks [PMID:39053695]. The umbrella review found 0.5-3 g/d in capsules for up to 3 months consistently effective [PMID:35147170]. Effects on BP were larger at >=2 g/d [PMID:42566667].

** Safety & adverse effects
- Generally well tolerated. Reported effects are heartburn, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea and mouth/throat irritation [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger]. Belching was noted in pregnancy trials [PMID:40808693].
- OA meta-analysis: people on ginger were more than twice as likely to stop treatment (RR 2.33, 1.04-5.22), mostly because of GI complaints [PMID:25300574].
- CINV RCTs reported no serious adverse events [PMID:37699474] and no safety difference vs placebo [PMID:28666335]. A 2026 SR noted two studies with worse CINV on ginger [PMID:42613614].
- Pregnancy:
  - RCT meta-analysis: no significant excess of spontaneous abortion vs placebo (RR 3.14, 0.65-15.11; wide CI) or vs vitamin B6 [PMID:24642205].
  - Norwegian MoBa cohort (n=68,522; 1,020 exposed): no increase in malformations, stillbirth/perinatal death, preterm birth, low birth weight or low Apgar [PMID:22706624].
    - Korean cohort of dried-ginger exposure (n=159): borderline stillbirth signal (OR 7.8, 0.9-70.3), confounded by co-exposure to other herbs [PMID:25093607].
  - EU HMPC monograph advises against use in pregnancy and breastfeeding because data are inadequate [URL:https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma].
- Bleeding:
  - Platelet evidence is equivocal [PMID:26488162]. A bleeding-risk review rates the evidence for ginger as conflicting [PMID:36304597].
  - Warfarin users: ginger was independently associated with self-reported bleeding (OR 3.20, 2.42-4.24) in a prospective cohort [PMID:17723077].
- Liver injury: no consistent signal in the retrieved literature. GGE03 trials reported no clinically significant laboratory changes [PMID:41599979] [PMID:40878144].

** Contraindications
- Allergy to ginger [URL:https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma].
- Pregnancy and lactation: regulators differ. EU HMPC advises against use for lack of adequate data [URL:https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma]. NCCIH says use in pregnancy "may be safe" and that little is known for breastfeeding [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger]. Cohort data are reassuring [PMID:22706624].
- Children: EU HMPC does not recommend use under 6 years for motion sickness or under 18 for other indications [URL:https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma].
- Active bleeding or upcoming surgery: a review advises stopping non-essential supplements 2 weeks before surgery [PMID:36304597].
- Gallstones: this caution is often repeated in secondary sources, but no retrieved primary source or regulator text (EMA, NCCIH) supports it, so no contraindication is asserted here.

** Interactions
*** Drugs
- Vitamin K antagonists (warfarin, phenprocoumon):
  - A crossover PK/PD study in 12 healthy men found no change in INR, S-/R-warfarin clearance or platelet aggregation [PMID:15801937].
  - Case report: phenprocoumon user reached INR 10 with epistaxis, causality "probable" [PMID:14742762].
  - Prospective warfarin cohort: bleeding OR 3.20 [PMID:17723077].
  - Reviews list ginger among herbs that increase bleeding risk [PMID:32478963]. A 2025 evidence review says even small quantities may potentiate warfarin [PMID:39288907].
  - Check INR more often.
- Direct oral anticoagulants: the anticoagulant review notes food-herb interactions may also matter for DOACs, but has no ginger-specific DOAC data [PMID:39288907].
- Antiplatelet agents and NSAIDs: ginger's antiplatelet effect is equivocal [PMID:26488162]. Zingiberaceae extracts and NSAIDs have both been linked to higher bleeding risk, with no comparative trials [PMID:25972154].
- Nifedipine: in hypertensive patients and volunteers, ginger potentiated nifedipine's antiplatelet effect (1 g ginger + 10 mg nifedipine) [PMID:16883626]. It may also add to BP lowering [PMID:42566667].
- Antidiabetic drugs, including metformin: FBS and HbA1c reductions in T2D [PMID:42337792] [PMID:35031435] mean glucose lowering may be additive. Monitor glucose. No direct interaction study.
- Antihypertensives: modest BP reduction [PMID:42566667], so additive effect is possible but minor.
- Tacrolimus:
  - A review lists ginger with grapefruit and turmeric as CYP3A4 inhibitors that may raise tacrolimus levels [PMID:36297591].
  - In a renal-transplant case, 10 g/d ginger for 4 days did not change tacrolimus trough levels [PMID:37586787].
  - In-vitro CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition provides the mechanism [PMID:37134183].
  - Monitor trough levels.
- CYP3A4/P-gp substrates with narrow therapeutic index, including oral kinase inhibitors: in-vitro inhibition [PMID:37134183] and a case report of a serious ginger-crizotinib pharmacokinetic interaction [PMID:30701569].
*** Other supplements
- Garlic: a bleeding review links garlic strongly to surgical bleeding and rates ginger evidence as conflicting [PMID:36304597]. Stacking adds exposure to bleeding risk.
- Turmeric: associated with bleeding in anticoagulated patients [PMID:36304597]. It shares putative CYP3A4 inhibition with ginger [PMID:36297591]. The two were co-tested in a tacrolimus case without effect [PMID:37586787].
- CoQ10: in warfarin users, CoQ10 (OR 3.69) and ginger (OR 3.20) were each independently associated with bleeding, and using more than one CAM product was itself a risk factor [PMID:17723077].
- Ginkgo biloba: listed with ginger among herbs that raise bleeding risk with warfarin [PMID:32478963]. Neither changed warfarin PK/PD in healthy men [PMID:15801937].
*** Conditions
- Pregnancy, lactation, children, perioperative period: see Contraindications.
- Diabetes, especially on glucose-lowering drugs: glucose may fall [PMID:42337792].
- Bleeding disorders: equivocal antiplatelet activity [PMID:26488162] [PMID:36304597].
*** Lab-test interference
- No assay artifact was found. Ginger may genuinely change INR in patients on vitamin K antagonists [PMID:14742762] [PMID:39288907], as well as glucose/HbA1c [PMID:42337792], lipids [PMID:36786398] and CRP [PMID:41123858].

** Latest research
- [2026-08] BP dose-response MA (12 RCTs): SBP -5.03, DBP -2.03 mmHg; GRADE low; larger effects at >=2 g/d [PMID:42566667].
- [2026-08] SR of ginger in oncology supportive care (24 studies, n=2409): heterogeneous; two studies found worse CINV [PMID:42613614].
- [2026-06] T2D cardiometabolic MA (13 RCTs): FBS -16.27 mg/dL, HbA1c -0.41%, TG -17.1 mg/dL; effects of limited clinical importance [PMID:42337792].
- [2026-05] PONV Bayesian NMA (54 RCTs, n=8275): ginger ranked best for nausea severity; GRADE low/very low [PMID:42095787].
- [2026-04] NVP NMA (24 RCTs, n=3017): ginger among effective non-pharmacological options; adverse-event reporting sparse [PMID:42075106].
- [2026-01] RCT of steamed ginger extract GGE03 (n=80): lower body fat, weight, TG and TC; no product-related AEs [PMID:41599979].
- [2025-11] SR/MA of CINV (35 RCTs): ginger capsules with antiemetics cut grade 3 acute nausea (RR 0.19) [PMID:38625733].
- [2025-11] Cardiovascular-biomarker SR/MA (41 RCTs): FBS -12.79 mg/dL, HbA1c -0.68%, SBP -2.72 mmHg [PMID:41195902].
- [2025-10] Inflammation dose-response MA (29 RCTs): CRP -0.86 mg/L; non-linear IL-6 dose-response [PMID:41123858].
- [2025-10] Obesity MA (36 RCTs): no effect on weight/BMI; small reductions in WC and body fat [PMID:41101746].
- [2025-09] RCT of GGE03 in functional dyspepsia (n=80): improved GSRS scores [PMID:40878144].
- [2025-08] Knee OA NMA (39 RCTs): ginger benefited some outcomes; Boswellia ranked highest [PMID:40806131].
- [2025-07] RCT (n=30): 125 mg/d extract improved joint pain/function and inflammatory markers [PMID:40732990].
- [2025-07] Review of meta-analyses: 500-1500 mg/d for NVP; belching as a side effect [PMID:40808693].
- [2024-12] Body-composition GRADE MA (27 RCTs): weight -1.52 kg, low/very low certainty [PMID:38261398].
- [2024-11] Dysmenorrhea MA: pain SMD -1.13, comparable to NSAIDs [PMID:38770631].
- [2024-10] T2D glycemic MA (5 RCTs): no significant pooled FBS/HbA1c effect [PMID:39053695].

** Open questions
- Clinical size of the bleeding interaction with anticoagulants. A controlled PK/PD study was null, but case reports and cohort data suggest risk.
- Optimal standardized product and dose. Gingerol/shogaol content varies, and trials use powders, extracts and steamed extracts interchangeably.
- Whether ginger adds benefit to modern NK1/5-HT3 antiemetic regimens for CINV. Large RCTs disagree.
- Whether the borderline stillbirth signal from one Korean cohort (confounded by other herbs) is real. Larger cohorts did not reproduce it.
- Whether in-vitro CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition translates into clinically relevant interactions in humans (e.g. tacrolimus, kinase inhibitors).
- Durability and clinical relevance of the small glycemic, lipid and BP effects. Most trials are short, small and highly heterogeneous.
- The gallstone contraindication found in secondary literature lacks a retrieved primary source.
