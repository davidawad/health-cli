** Identity & forms
- Likely product: Blueprint "NAC + Ginger + Curcumin" capsules. A supplement database lists, per 3-capsule serving: N-acetyl-L-cysteine 1200 mg, ginger extract 400 mg (standardized to 26% gingeroids) and curcumin extract 250 mg. It lists 30 servings per container, yet the directions say "1 capsule daily with meals" [URL:https://supp.co/products/blueprint-nac-ginger-curcumin]. A retailer lists 90 capsules with one capsule daily [URL:https://healf.com/en-uk/products/blueprint-nac-capsules]. Neither listing names a curcumin delivery form or piperine, so the curcumin's bioavailability is unknown. Treat this as a generic NAC + ginger + curcumin combination.
- No randomized trial of this three-ingredient product, or of any NAC + ginger + curcumin combination, was found. A PubMed search for acetylcysteine AND curcumin AND ginger returned 0 records on 2026-10-13. The evidence below is ingredient-level and cannot be transferred directly to the blend.
- N-acetylcysteine (NAC): acetylated cysteine. It is a prescription mucolytic and the acetaminophen-overdose antidote. Oral doses of 200-400 mg reach peak plasma levels of 0.35-4 mg/L within 1-2 h. Terminal half-life is about 6.25 h, and roughly 70% of clearance is nonrenal [PMID:2029805]. Oral bioavailability depends on the formulation [PMID:39430194].
- Ginger (Zingiber officinale rhizome): gingerols and shogaols. Trials mostly used 0.5-2 g/day of powder or extract [PMID:39053695] [PMID:24642205].
- Curcumin: the main curcuminoid of turmeric (Curcuma longa). Plain curcumin is poorly bioavailable. With 2 g curcumin alone, human serum levels were undetectable or very low, and 20 mg piperine raised bioavailability by 2000% [PMID:9619120]. Piperine and other enhanced formulations are common in curcumin products [PMID:36720711].
- Combination pharmacology: one rat nanoparticle study found that NAC-modified carriers raised curcumin AUC 3.25-fold [PMID:32763460]. This is preclinical and formulation-specific, and not evidence that adding NAC to a capsule boosts curcumin absorption.

** Mechanisms
- NAC supplies cysteine for glutathione (GSH) synthesis and breaks mucus disulfide bonds [PMID:2029805] [PMID:39632267]. With glycine (GlyNAC), it corrected GSH deficiency and oxidative stress in a 16-week RCT in 24 older adults [PMID:35975308]. Even at 6 g/day orally, brain GSH did not rise, possibly because of low oral bioavailability [PMID:28940353]. A COPD meta-analysis found no change in GSH levels [PMID:42473447].
- NAC donates sulfhydryl groups, which potentiates nitroglycerin vasodilation (possibly via S-nitroso-NAC) and its antiplatelet effect [PMID:3137075] [PMID:9676716]. A review warns that very high NAC concentrations may cause "reductive stress" [PMID:39430194].
- Ginger lowers CRP, TNF-alpha and IL-6 and raises antioxidant capacity in RCT meta-analyses [PMID:41123858]. Antiplatelet activity is dose- and preparation-dependent: 2 g dried ginger had no effect on platelet function or bleeding time in a small RCT [PMID:8165628].
- Curcumin is anti-inflammatory and antioxidant (CRP, TNF-alpha, IL-6, MDA, SOD) [PMID:36804260]. Preclinical work shows antiplatelet, anticoagulant and fibrinolytic activity [PMID:29052850]. In vitro, curcumin inhibits CYP2C9 (IC50 4.3 uM) and CYP3A4 (IC50 16.3 uM), but low systemic exposure limits hepatic relevance [PMID:17433521]. Piperine inhibits glucuronidation [PMID:9619120], P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4 [PMID:34776989].

** Dosing in trials
- Product: per-serving amounts of 1200 mg NAC, 400 mg ginger extract and 250 mg curcumin extract are listed, but the 3-capsule serving conflicts with the "1 capsule daily" directions [URL:https://supp.co/products/blueprint-nac-ginger-curcumin].
- NAC, COPD: 600 mg twice daily for 2 years in the largest recent RCT [PMID:39349461]. A meta-analysis found benefit at <=600 mg/day (RR 0.84) but not at >600 mg/day (RR 0.88, NS) [PMID:42473447].
- NAC, psychiatry: 1000-3000 mg/day for 8-24 weeks in depression trials [PMID:39504621]. In the IPF genotype trial (PRECISIONS), 600 mg three times daily [PMID:36514019].
- NAC, sport/high dose: a typical 5.8 g/day (range 1.2-20 g/day), with side effects appearing to rise with dose [PMID:28102488].
- Ginger: 1.2-2 g/day powder for 4-12 weeks in T2DM [PMID:39053695]. Lower doses (<1500 mg/day) favored for nausea of pregnancy [PMID:24642205]. Blood pressure effects were larger at >=2000 mg/day [PMID:42566667].
- Curcumin: OA benefit is suggested at >12 weeks of use [PMID:34017975]. Combination trials used 600 mg curcumin + 15 mg gingerol + 7.5 mg piperine daily (migraine) [PMID:42021338] and 500 mg-1 g/day ginger + curcumin (1-week diet study) [PMID:42530541].

** Safety & adverse effects
- NAC (oral): nausea, vomiting and diarrhea are the commonest effects [PMID:2029805]. Side-effect odds versus placebo were 1.11 (0.88-1.39) [PMID:28102488]. Mucolytics did not increase adverse events in the Cochrane review [PMID:31107966] or the 2026 NAC meta-analysis [PMID:42473447].
  - Inhaled acetylcysteine (prescription solution) can cause bronchospasm. The label says asthmatics "should be watched carefully" and treatment should stop if bronchospasm progresses [URL:https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=4101]. This applies to inhaled, not oral capsule, use.
- Ginger: abdominal discomfort, heartburn and diarrhea [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger]. In an OA meta-analysis, ginger takers were more likely to withdraw than placebo takers (RR 2.33) [PMID:25300574]. No significant excess of miscarriage versus placebo (RR 3.14, 0.65-15.11; wide CI) [PMID:24642205].
- Curcumin/turmeric: GI upset, nausea and diarrhea [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric]. OA trials show adverse-event rates similar to placebo and 12% lower than NSAIDs [PMID:33511486].
- Curcumin/turmeric hepatotoxicity (idiosyncratic herb-induced liver injury):
  - US DILIN: 10 cases, all since 2011, 9 hepatocellular. Latency was 1-4 months; 5 patients were hospitalized and 1 died. 3 of 7 tested products contained piperine. HLA-B*35:01 carrier frequency was 0.45 vs 0.056-0.069 in controls [PMID:36252717].
  - Histopathology series: 11 cases (91% female), mostly acute pan-lobular or zone-3 hepatitis. All normalized after stopping [PMID:39381846].
  - Italy (Tuscany): 7 cases, all linked to high-bioavailability, high-dose formulations [PMID:32656820].
  - Recurrence on rechallenge with a turmeric + black pepper product: drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis needing steroids [PMID:42160492]. A further case was linked to a turmeric-piperine supplement [PMID:41281037].
  - USP (2026): reactions are rare and resolve on withdrawal, but acute liver failure has occurred. USP added a cautionary liver statement to its turmeric/curcuminoid monographs [PMID:42364655]. NCCIH notes liver damage with bioavailable formulations [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric].
  - Piperine is suspected as a contributor, through inhibition of drug metabolism. A RUCAM-based review argues causality for curcumin itself is weak [PMID:34776989].
- Turmeric/curcumin is the most commonly used of the 6 botanicals most often reported as hepatotoxic in the US (n=236 of the NHANES users) [PMID:39102266].
- Bleeding: in a review, ginger's overall bleeding risk was conflicting, and turmeric was associated with bleeding only in people on anticoagulants [PMID:36304597].

** Contraindications
- Prior turmeric/curcumin-associated liver injury: recurrence was more severe on re-exposure [PMID:42160492]. Existing liver disease warrants caution, per the USP cautionary statement [PMID:42364655].
- Pregnancy: NCCIH says turmeric supplements "may be unsafe" in pregnancy [URL:https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric]. Ginger alone has supportive data [PMID:24642205]. No data exist for the combination.
- Asthma/bronchospasm history: this applies to the inhaled acetylcysteine product only [URL:https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=4101]. No signal was found for oral capsules.
- Scheduled surgery: a review advises stopping non-essential supplements 2 weeks before surgery [PMID:36304597].
- Hypersensitivity to any ingredient.

** Interactions
*** Drugs
- Nitroglycerin/organic nitrates: in an RCT of IV NTG + IV NAC (5 g every 6 h) in unstable angina, symptomatic hypotension occurred in 7 of 24 patients vs 0 of 22 [PMID:3127076] [PMID:3137075]. IV NAC also potentiated NTG coronary vasodilation [PMID:9676716]. The doses were far above supplement doses, so relevance to 1.2 g oral NAC is uncertain.
- Warfarin/oral anticoagulants: an evidence review says ginger, even in small quantities, can potentiate warfarin [PMID:39288907]. A systematic review lists ginger among herbs raising bleeding risk with warfarin [PMID:32478963]. However, ginger did not alter warfarin PK/INR in a crossover study in 12 healthy men [PMID:15801937]. Meriva curcumin did not change INR with warfarin or dabigatran over 10 days [PMID:30070343]. Turmeric is linked to bleeding in anticoagulated people [PMID:36304597]. Monitor INR.
- Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticlopidine): Meriva curcumin did not change bleeding time over 10 days [PMID:30070343]. Antiplatelet effects are mainly preclinical [PMID:29052850]. 2 g dried ginger did not affect platelets [PMID:8165628].
- Tacrolimus and other CYP3A4 substrates: one case report of turmeric-associated tacrolimus toxicity (trough 29.9 ng/mL) with acute nephrotoxicity [PMID:28104136]. In vitro, curcumin inhibits CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 [PMID:17433521]. Piperine inhibits CYP3A4 and P-gp [PMID:34776989]. Monitor trough levels of narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.
- Antidiabetics (metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin): curcumin and ginger both lower FBG/HbA1c in T2DM [PMID:39683570] [PMID:42337792], so additive effects are possible. A 10-day study found no interaction of Meriva with metformin [PMID:30070343].
- Activated charcoal: charcoal adsorbs acetylcysteine in vitro [URL:https://labeling.pfizer.com/ShowLabeling.aspx?id=4101] [PMID:2029805]. A 3-volunteer study found no significant drop in NAC absorption [PMID:7258201]. NAC reduced charcoal adsorption of acetaminophen and salicylate in vitro [PMID:11714205].
- Hepatotoxic drugs: no interaction study exists. Most published turmeric DILI cases involved concomitant medications [PMID:32656820] [PMID:42364655].
*** Other supplements
- Duplication: the corpus items N-Acetyl-L-Cysteine (NAC), Turmeric, Curcumin and Ginger Root contain the same actives. Stacking raises total NAC dose (side effects rise with dose [PMID:28102488]) and total curcuminoid exposure (liver injury with high-dose, high-bioavailability products [PMID:32656820]).
- Hepatotoxic botanical stack: Green Tea Extract and Ashwagandha share the 6-botanical hepatotoxicity list with turmeric [PMID:39102266]. USP also requires a liver caution for green tea extract [PMID:32140423].
- Bleeding stack: Garlic is strongly associated with surgical bleeding [PMID:36304597]. EPA/DHA/DPA: no overall bleeding excess, except a small rise with high-dose purified EPA [PMID:38742535]. Aspirin: see Drugs [PMID:30070343].
- Piperine/black pepper extract: enhances curcumin bioavailability [PMID:9619120] and is suspected in liver injury [PMID:34776989] [PMID:42160492].
*** Conditions
- Liver disease or prior herb-induced liver injury: see Contraindications [PMID:42364655].
- Diabetes on glucose-lowering drugs: possible additive lowering [PMID:39683570].
- Bleeding disorders or anticoagulation: caution [PMID:36304597].
- Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: NAC benefit or harm may depend on TOLLIP genotype [PMID:26331942].
*** Lab-test interference
- Creatinine: NAC lowers measured serum creatinine (WMD -2.80 umol/L) without changing cystatin C. The drop is larger with enzymatic (non-Jaffe) assays and IV NAC (-31.1 umol/L), which suggests analytic interference [PMID:33615065].
- Trinder-based assays (Roche): NAC causes >=10% inhibition of triglycerides (570 mg/L), cholesterol (740 mg/L), enzymatic creatinine (790 mg/L), uric acid and HDL-C. These concentrations exceed those expected with therapeutic dosing [PMID:26500003]. Interference with oral supplement doses is therefore unlikely.
- Salicylate assays: no cited source found for NAC interference.
- Liver tests: curcumin can cause true elevations (DILI), not assay artifacts [PMID:36252717].

** Genotype notes
- HLA-B*35:01: 7 of 10 DILIN turmeric liver-injury cases carried this allele (allele frequency 0.450 vs 0.056-0.069 in controls) [PMID:36252717]. The USP review notes emerging genetic susceptibility [PMID:42364655].
- TOLLIP rs3750920 (IPF): in PANTHER-IPF, NAC lowered composite-endpoint risk in TT carriers (HR 0.14, 0.02-0.83) but trended toward harm in CC carriers (HR 3.23, 0.79-13.16). The finding was replicated in an independent cohort [PMID:26331942]. The PRECISIONS phase III trial is testing 1800 mg/day NAC in TT patients [PMID:36514019].

** Open questions
- No trial of the NAC + ginger + curcumin combination exists. Additive or synergistic benefit is unproven, and the product's curcumin form and enhancer status are undisclosed.
- The serving size on the label (3 capsules vs 1 capsule) needs clarification. NAC dose (400 vs 1200 mg/day) matters for COPD evidence, where benefit was seen at <=600 mg/day [PMID:42473447].
- Incidence of turmeric/curcumin liver injury, and whether risk differs by formulation (piperine, nanoparticle, phytosome) or HLA-B*35:01 status [PMID:36252717] [PMID:34776989].
- Clinical relevance of the NAC-nitrate interaction at oral supplement doses. Human data are from IV dosing [PMID:3127076].
- Whether the oral NAC rise in GSH translates into clinical outcomes in healthy adults [PMID:28940353] [PMID:42473447].
- Heterogeneity (I2 >80%) and small, short, regionally clustered trials limit most curcumin and ginger metabolic MAs [PMID:41656101] [PMID:42337792].
