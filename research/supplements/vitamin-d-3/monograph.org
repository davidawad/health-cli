** Identity & forms
- Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form made in skin and the form used in most large modern trials (VITAL, D-Health, DO-HEALTH, D2d) [PMID:30415629] [PMID:35026158] [PMID:33170239] [PMID:31173679]. Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) is the plant/fungal form, and is also sold as a prescription product [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0d230fd8-2ea7-4dd6-a506-5f7804e539fc].
- D3 vs D2: a 2012 meta-analysis of RCTs found D3 raised serum 25(OH)D more than D2. The difference was clear with bolus dosing and disappeared with daily dosing [PMID:22552031]. A 2024 meta-analysis (20 comparisons) found that even with daily dosing measured by LC-MS/MS, D2 raised total 25(OH)D 10.39 nmol/L less than D3 (95% CI -14.62 to -6.16). Body mass index was the strongest modifier, and the difference was lost at BMI >25 kg/m2 [PMID:37865222].
- D2 lowers circulating 25(OH)D3, and D3 lowers 25(OH)D2, by a similar amount. In one RCT, D2 lowered day-28 25(OH)D3 by 13.2 nmol/L versus placebo [PMID:30658603].
- Other forms: calcifediol (25(OH)D3) and calcitriol (1,25(OH)2D3, prescription). A 2024 international consensus prefers oral cholecalciferol for supplementation and limits calcitriol to disorders where the active metabolite is not made adequately [PMID:38676447].
- Reference intakes (US IOM 2011): RDA 600 IU/day for ages 1-70 years and in pregnancy/lactation, and 800 IU/day above age 70. A serum 25(OH)D of at least 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) is sufficient for practically everyone, and there "may be reason for concern" above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13050/chapter/2].

** Mechanisms
- Vitamin D is hydroxylated in the liver (CYP2R1, a 25-hydroxylase) to 25(OH)D, the circulating status marker. It is then converted by 1-alpha-hydroxylase to calcitriol, which acts on the vitamin D receptor. Vitamin D binding protein (GC gene) carries the metabolites [PMID:28575224] [PMID:39337491].
- The classic action is calcium homeostasis. Vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption, renal calcium reabsorption and bone resorption, which is why excess causes hypercalcemia [PMID:34774245].
- CYP24A1 breaks down vitamin D metabolites. Granulomatous disease (for example sarcoidosis) can overexpress 1-alpha-hydroxylase outside the kidney, so supplements can produce hypercalcemia with high calcitriol (review and case report) [PMID:39337491] [PMID:36221396].
- Proposed effects outside bone (immune, beta-cell, telomere maintenance) rest mainly on mechanistic and observational data. Their size in trials is described below [PMID:41707752] [PMID:38676447].

** Dosing in trials
- VITAL: 2000 IU/day D3 for a median of 5.3 years in adults aged 50/55 and older [PMID:30415629].
- DO-HEALTH: 2000 IU/day D3 for 3 years in adults aged 70 and older [PMID:33170239].
- D-Health: 60,000 IU once a month for up to 5 years. Achieved 25(OH)D was 115 vs 77 nmol/L [PMID:35026158].
- D2d: 4000 IU/day. Achieved 25(OH)D was 54.3 ng/mL vs 28.8 ng/mL [PMID:31173679]. Prediabetes trials also used 20,000 IU/week [PMID:36745886].
- Annual bolus: 500,000 IU cholecalciferol once a year (Sanders). Levels rose to about 120 nmol/L at 1 month [PMID:20460620].
- Military: 800 IU D3 plus 2000 mg calcium daily during 8 weeks of training [PMID:18433305].
- D-Lay MS: 100,000 IU every 2 weeks for 24 months [PMID:40063041].
- Endocrine Society 2024 guideline: for people over 50 in whom vitamin D is indicated, it suggests daily rather than intermittent high doses. It suggests against empiric doses above the DRI in healthy adults under 75 and against routine 25(OH)D testing. It states that optimal doses and target 25(OH)D levels for disease prevention are unclear [PMID:38828931].

** Safety & adverse effects
- Upper limits (IOM 2011): 4000 IU/day from age 9 years, including pregnancy and lactation. Lower limits apply in infants and children: 1000 IU (0-6 months), 1500 IU (6-12 months), 2500 IU (1-3 years) and 3000 IU (4-8 years) [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13050/chapter/2].
- Toxicity: hypervitaminosis D is rare and usually comes from misuse of high-dose OTC products or prescription errors. It shows up as hypercalcemia [PMID:32491799]. Susceptibility varies between people, partly through CYP24A1 variants, and hypercalcemia can be refractory to standard treatment [PMID:39337491].
- At usual trial doses, large RCTs did not find excess hypercalcemia or other adverse events in VITAL [PMID:30415629]. D-Health found no difference in hypercalcemia or kidney stones [PMID:34337905]. In the prediabetes IPD meta-analysis, adverse-event rate ratios had wide confidence intervals: kidney stones 1.17 (0.69-1.99), hypercalcemia 2.34 (0.83-6.66), hypercalciuria 1.65 (0.83-3.28) [PMID:36745886].
- Kidney stones with calcium: in the WHI, calcium 1000 mg plus D3 400 IU/day increased kidney stones (HR 1.17, 1.02-1.34) [PMID:16481635]. In a separate trial of vitamin D and calcium in older women, hypercalciuria occurred in 30.6% and hypercalcemia in 8.8%. Neither was dose-related, and hypercalciuria was equally common with placebo [PMID:24937025].
- High-dose bolus harm: annual 500,000 IU increased falls (IRR 1.15, 1.02-1.30) and fractures (IRR 1.26, 1.00-1.59) [PMID:20460620]. A meta-analysis of intermittent or single high doses found a borderline increase in falls with D3 [PMID:37120684]. The 2024 Endocrine Society systematic review concluded that high-dose intermittent dosing may increase falls compared with daily dosing [PMID:38828942]. In D-Health, monthly 60,000 IU did not increase fractures [PMID:37011645], but falls rose in people with BMI <25 (OR 1.25, 1.09-1.43) [PMID:34337905]. D-Health exploratory analyses also hinted at higher cancer mortality after year 2 (HR 1.24, 1.01-1.54), and the authors suggested precaution with this regimen in vitamin D-replete people [PMID:35026158].

** Contraindications
- Hypercalcemia or hypervitaminosis D (label contraindication for prescription ergocalciferol) [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0d230fd8-2ea7-4dd6-a506-5f7804e539fc].
- Sarcoidosis and other granulomatous disease: extrarenal 1-alpha-hydroxylase activity can cause hypercalcemia. There is a case report of hypercalcemia with acute kidney injury after a year of cholecalciferol [PMID:36221396] [PMID:39337491]. Major trials excluded people with sarcoidosis, hypercalcemia, hyperparathyroidism or kidney stones [PMID:37380191].
- Primary hyperparathyroidism: a meta-analysis (11 studies, 388 patients) found that supplementing deficient patients lowered PTH without causing hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria. This supports caution with monitoring rather than absolute avoidance [PMID:33453021].

** Interactions
*** Drugs
- Thiazide diuretics: thiazides reduce urinary calcium excretion. With vitamin D (labels for ergocalciferol in hypoparathyroidism, and calcitriol) they can cause hypercalcemia, and thiazide hypercalcemia can unmask hyperparathyroidism [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75] [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0d230fd8-2ea7-4dd6-a506-5f7804e539fc] [PMID:34774245].
- Digoxin/digitalis: hypercalcemia can precipitate arrhythmias in people taking digitalis (calcitriol label) [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75].
- Orlistat: in adolescents taking orlistat, vitamin D levels fell after 1 month despite a multivitamin [PMID:12126214]. Mineral oil also interferes with absorption of fat-soluble vitamins [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0d230fd8-2ea7-4dd6-a506-5f7804e539fc].
- Cholestyramine (bile acid sequestrants): reduces intestinal absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, per the calcitriol label [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75].
- Enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants: phenytoin and phenobarbital speed up metabolism of 25(OH)D3 (label) [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75]. In a meta-analysis, carbamazepine users had 25(OH)D about 4.0 ng/mL lower [PMID:34847425].
- Corticosteroids: they inhibit calcium absorption, which is functional antagonism with vitamin D (label) [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75]. Conversely, glucocorticoids are used to treat vitamin D intoxication [PMID:39337491].
- Calcitriol and other active vitamin D analogues: the calcitriol label says to withhold pharmacological doses of vitamin D during calcitriol therapy because of additive hypercalcemia [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75].
*** Other supplements
- Calcium: when combined with vitamin D, kidney stones increased (WHI) [PMID:16481635]. For fractures, the combination gave RR 0.91 (0.84-0.99) but with absolute benefit judged not meaningful [PMID:42161415]. Stress fractures fell in military recruits [PMID:18433305]. The calcitriol label warns against uncontrolled extra calcium [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75].
- Magnesium: in an RCT, magnesium supplementation raised 25(OH)D3 when baseline 25(OH)D was about 30 ng/mL and lowered it at higher baselines [PMID:30541089]. In a small RCT in people with overweight or obesity, magnesium plus vitamin D raised 25(OH)D more than vitamin D alone [PMID:35576873].
- Vitamin K2: the proposed rationale is that vitamin K-dependent proteins direct calcium to bone rather than arteries. This is mechanistic only; combined MK-7 plus D3 did not slow aortic valve or coronary calcification in the AVADEC trial (narrative review) [PMID:42654291].
- Boron: a narrative review claims boron "beneficially impacts" vitamin D use. No human RCT on this interaction was found, so the evidence is very weak [PMID:26770156].
- Fish Oil (Omega-3): commonly co-tested (VITAL, DO-HEALTH). There was no clinical interaction, but the factorial arms appeared additive for autoimmune disease in VITAL and for PhenoAge in DO-HEALTH [PMID:35082139] [PMID:39900648].
- Stacking with other vitamin D-containing products (multivitamins, fortified foods) adds to total intake, which matters relative to the 4000 IU/day UL [URL:https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13050/chapter/2].
*** Conditions
- Sarcoidosis/granulomatous disease, hypercalcemia, primary hyperparathyroidism: see Contraindications.
- History of kidney stones: an exclusion criterion in D-Health. Stone risk rose with calcium plus D in the WHI [PMID:37380191] [PMID:16481635].
- Obesity: smaller 25(OH)D response, and the D2 vs D3 difference shrinks at BMI >25 [PMID:37865222].
- Chronic kidney disease: the calcitriol label notes that hypercalcemia can raise creatinine, and that magnesium antacids cause hypermagnesemia in dialysis patients on calcitriol. Activation of native D3 depends on renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation [URL:https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=261398eb-80b8-4c3d-9d2b-3a8004347a75].
*** Lab-test interference
- 25(OH)D immunoassays: older immunoassays overestimated 25(OH)D3 and detected less than 50% of 25(OH)D2 compared with HPLC. People taking D2 may therefore appear falsely low, and assay-specific decision limits are needed [PMID:16390606]. Assay design (binding-protein capture, cross-reactivity with 3-epi-25(OH)D3, heterophile antibody interference) affects accuracy [PMID:24887958]. Lack of standardization still makes it hard to compare 25(OH)D thresholds across studies [PMID:38676447]. LC-MS/MS measures D2 and D3 metabolites separately [PMID:37865222].

** Genotype notes
- GC (vitamin D binding protein) and CYP2R1 (25-hydroxylase) variants modify how much 25(OH)D rises with supplementation:
  - In an antenatal cholecalciferol RCT, achieved 25(OH)D was associated with CYP2R1 rs10741657 (beta -5.2 nmol/L) and GC rs2282679 (beta +4.2 nmol/L) [PMID:28575224].
  - In a prediabetes RCT (1600 IU/day for 24 weeks), CYP2R1 rs12794714 AA and GC rs4588 GA carriers had smaller 25(OH)D3 increases [PMID:40114038].
- VDR SNPs were not associated with tibial bone measures in British Army recruits [PMID:40324208].
- CYP24A1 variants may predispose to vitamin D toxicity (review) [PMID:39337491].
- No cited evidence supports genotype-guided dosing for clinical outcomes.

** Latest research
- [2026-08] A narrative review of omega-3, D3 and K2 reports that combined MK-7 plus D3 did not slow valve or coronary calcification in AVADEC, and calls the K2 rationale biologically plausible but unconfirmed in humans [PMID:42654291].
- [2026-05] A BMJ SR/MA of 69 RCTs (153,902 people) found vitamin D alone has no effect on any fracture (RR 1.00, high certainty). Combined calcium plus D gave RR 0.91, with little to no clinically meaningful benefit for fractures or falls [PMID:42161415].
- [2026-05] A mini-review of vitamin D for diabetes prevention notes that the IPD meta-analysis showed a 15% relative risk reduction, with greater benefit at low baseline 25(OH)D or BMI <30 [PMID:41707752].
- [2025-09] A dose-response meta-analysis of observational studies found the lowest vs highest vitamin D category had RR 1.49 for dementia. That works out to a 1.2% lower risk per 10 nmol/L, which is not causal evidence [PMID:41001202].
- [2025-08] DO-HEALTH found 2000 IU/day D3 did not reduce incident vertebral fractures over 3 years [PMID:40492704].
- [2025-07] VITAL telomere substudy: 2000 IU/day D3 reduced leukocyte telomere attrition by 0.14 kb over 4 years [PMID:40409468].
- [2025-06] A British Army recruit RCT found no association of 25(OH)D or VDR SNPs with tibial density or microarchitecture [PMID:40324208].
- [2025-06] D-Health cancer incidence: HR 1.02 (0.95-1.10). The authors call this convincing evidence of no effect on cancer incidence [PMID:40096917].
- [2025-04] D-Lay MS RCT: 100,000 IU every 2 weeks reduced disease activity after clinically isolated syndrome (HR 0.66), driven by MRI outcomes. Relapse did not differ significantly [PMID:40063041].
- [2025-04] Jolliffe updated SR/MA of acute respiratory infections: OR 0.94 (0.88-1.00), no longer statistically significant [PMID:39993397].
- [2025-04] DO-HEALTH: no effect of D3 on muscle mass or incident sarcopenia [PMID:39565152].
- [2025-03] DO-HEALTH epigenetic clocks: omega-3 slowed several clocks. Vitamin D contributed only an additive effect on PhenoAge [PMID:39900648].
- [2025-01] A critical meta-analysis of respiratory infections found that benefit is confined to small trials. Trials of 248 or more participants gave SOR 0.98 (0.94-1.03) [PMID:39808630].
- [2024-11] A meta-analysis of healthy older adults found no reduction in total fractures and a higher hip fracture risk in women (RR 1.34) [PMID:38997531].
- [2024-11] A dose-response MA of depression (31 RCTs) found SMD -0.32 per 1000 IU/day, attenuating in trials longer than 24 weeks [PMID:39552387].
- [2024-09] An international consensus ("Controversies in Vitamin D") says optimal 25(OH)D is still debated and assay standardization is lacking, and prefers cholecalciferol [PMID:38676447].
- [2024-07] Endocrine Society guideline: empiric vitamin D is suggested for ages 1-18, adults over 75, pregnancy and high-risk prediabetes. It suggests against doses above the DRI in healthy adults under 75 and against routine 25(OH)D testing, and sets no target level [PMID:38828931] [PMID:38828942].
- [2024-07] DO-HEALTH: a small total-hip BMD benefit only [PMID:38613445].
- [2024-06] VITAL 2-year post-trial follow-up: the autoimmune benefit of vitamin D faded (HR 0.98 at 7 years) [PMID:38272846].
- [2024-04] UK Biobank cohort: regular vitamin D use was associated with lower Alzheimer's disease risk (HR 0.83). This is observational [PMID:38296029].
- [2024-02] DO-HEALTH: no reduction in major cardiovascular events (aHR 1.37, 0.88-2.14) [PMID:38199870].

** Open questions
- What 25(OH)D target, if any, matters for prevention outside bone? The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline declined to set one [PMID:38828931], while other consensus groups still debate higher targets [PMID:38676447].
- Is the cancer-mortality benefit real and specific to daily dosing [PMID:37004841], given that D-Health's monthly bolus pointed the other way [PMID:35026158]?
- Does vitamin D benefit people who are truly deficient? Most large trials enrolled largely replete participants [PMID:38676447].
- Is the hip fracture signal in healthy older women real [PMID:38997531]? Is the falls signal with bolus dosing at normal BMI real [PMID:34337905]?
- Do the telomere effects (VITAL) translate into clinical aging outcomes, given null results with monthly bolus dosing (D-Health) [PMID:40409468] [PMID:37702332]?
- No RCT evidence links vitamin D supplementation to dementia prevention. Cohort associations may be confounded [PMID:34853363] [PMID:38296029].
